Pick up a strip of Glycomet, Telma or Thyronorm and look near the edge. There is a small box with a red line and the letters Rx, and beside it the words "Schedule H drug. Warning: To be sold by retail on the prescription of a Registered Medical Practitioner only." Almost every long-term medicine in India carries that box. This guide is about what it means, and why a pharmacist reads your prescription before a Schedule H medicine leaves the shelf.

## What Schedule H is

India's medicines are governed by the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and the Rules made under it in 1945. The Rules sort medicines into schedules, which are simply lists. Schedule H is the list of medicines that can only be sold against a doctor's prescription. It runs to hundreds of molecules: antibiotics, blood pressure medicines, diabetes tablets, thyroid hormone, steroids, antidepressants, and many more.

A medicine ends up on Schedule H not because it is dangerous in the way poison is dangerous, but because getting the choice or the dose wrong has consequences that a person cannot easily see for themselves. A blood pressure tablet at the wrong dose does not hurt today; it shows up in a reading months later. An antibiotic taken for a viral cold does nothing for the cold and makes the next infection harder to treat. The prescription requirement puts a doctor's judgement between the medicine and the person, once, at the start.

## What Schedule H1 adds

In 2013 the government carved out a shorter list, Schedule H1, for medicines where misuse worries the regulator more: newer antibiotics kept in reserve, some anti-tuberculosis drugs, and habit-forming medicines such as zolpidem and certain sedatives. For these, the pharmacy must also keep a register with the patient's name, the doctor's name and the quantity sold, and hold that register for three years. On the strip you will see H1 instead of H. The check is the same; the paperwork is longer.

## What the law asks of the pharmacy

Rule 65 is the part of the Rules that governs how a retail pharmacy sells medicine. For a Schedule H medicine it requires three things: a prescription from a registered medical practitioner, a registered pharmacist supervising the sale, and a record of the sale. The Pharmacy Practice Regulations of 2015 go further and describe the pharmacist's job in plain terms: to review the prescription, to check the dose and the possible interactions, to counsel the patient, and to refuse to dispense if something is wrong.

The pharmacist's job is not to approve you. It is to catch the one thing on the paper that should not go out as written.

## What the pharmacist is actually looking at

When a pharmacist reads your prescription before dispensing, they are checking a short list, quickly, from experience.

Is it a prescription? It needs the doctor's name, their registration number, a date, and the medicine written clearly enough to read. A photo of the old strip is not a prescription, though it can help the pharmacist understand what you mean.

Is it current? A prescription for a long-term medicine is generally treated as good for six months. After that the doctor should have seen you again, because doses change.

Does the dose make sense? A strength that does not exist, a frequency that is unusual, or a quantity that does not match the course are the commonest slips. Most are a doctor's handwriting, not a doctor's error, and a phone call fixes them.

Does anything clash? Two medicines that should not be taken together, or a medicine that is wrong for someone with a particular condition the prescription mentions. This is where the pharmacist earns the registration number on the counter.

Is the substitution right? If the pharmacy does not have the brand written, the pharmacist can offer another brand of the same molecule, strength and form, but only with your agreement, and never a different molecule. If you say no, the answer is no.

## What happens when something is wrong

Usually, a message. The pharmacist asks for a clearer photo, or the second page, or the doctor's registration number. Sometimes a call to the doctor's clinic. Occasionally, a refusal: the medicine is not on the prescription, or the prescription is too old, or the dose is one the pharmacist is not willing to dispense without hearing from the doctor. That refusal is the system working. Nothing is charged for an order that is not dispensed.

## Why the check is done by a named person

Every pharmacist in India holds a registration number from a State Pharmacy Council, and that number is what stands behind a dispensing decision. When you see "Checked by Anil Mehta, Registered Pharmacist, Reg no. PB-45821" on an order, that is not a design flourish. It is a statement of who took responsibility for the medicine leaving the shelf, and it can be looked up.

## What you can do to make it quick

Send the whole prescription, both sides if there are two. Make sure the doctor's name and the date are in the frame. Tell the pharmacist about any other medicines you take, including things bought without a prescription. And if a medicine you have taken for years is questioned, do not take it personally. The pharmacist is reading the paper, not judging you.
