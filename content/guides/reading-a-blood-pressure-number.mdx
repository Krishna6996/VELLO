A blood pressure reading is two numbers with a line between them, like 128/82, and a unit almost nobody says out loud: millimetres of mercury, written mmHg. Most people know that lower is better and that 120/80 is somewhere near normal. This guide is about what the numbers actually measure, how to take a reading at home that means something, and how to read the result without frightening yourself.

## What the two numbers are

Your heart is a pump that squeezes and relaxes about seventy times a minute. Each squeeze pushes blood into the arteries and the pressure inside them rises. Between squeezes the heart refills and the pressure falls.

The top number, the systolic, is the pressure at the peak of the squeeze. The bottom number, the diastolic, is the pressure at the bottom of the rest. So 128/82 means the pressure in your arteries swings between 82 and 128 mmHg with every heartbeat.

Both numbers matter. Doctors have historically paid more attention to the top number in people over fifty, because arteries stiffen with age and the systolic tends to climb, and it is the systolic that most closely tracks the risk of stroke and heart attack. In younger people the diastolic is often the one that rises first.

## What counts as high

The thresholds differ a little between guidelines, which is one reason the internet is confusing on this point. The Indian Guidelines on Hypertension and the European guidelines both call 140/90 or above hypertension when it is measured in a clinic. The American guideline sets the line lower, at 130/80. All of them agree on the broad picture: below 120/80 is where risk is lowest, the range between about 120 and 140 on top is worth watching and worth changing habits for, and 140/90 and above, measured properly and more than once, usually needs treatment.

Notice the phrase "more than once". Blood pressure moves all day. It rises when you climb stairs, drink coffee, argue, or sit in a waiting room. A single reading of 150/95 taken after rushing to a clinic tells the doctor very little. A week of readings taken calmly at home tells them a great deal.

## How to take a reading your doctor can trust

The guidelines are specific about this, and the details are not fussy for their own sake. Each one removes a known source of error.

Sit in a chair with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor, for five minutes, before you start. Do not talk during the reading.

Rest your arm on a table so that the cuff is at the level of your heart. An arm hanging down reads high; an arm held up reads low.

Use a cuff that fits. The inflatable part should wrap around about 80 percent of your upper arm. A cuff that is too small reads high by 10 mmHg or more. Most home monitors come with a medium cuff; if your arm is large or small, buy the right size.

Take the reading before your morning medicines, before tea or coffee, and not within half an hour of exercise or a cigarette. Empty your bladder first.

Take two readings a minute apart and write both down, with the date and time. If they differ by more than 10, take a third.

Do this every morning for a week before a doctor's appointment, and again for a week whenever your medicine changes. Take the sheet with you.

## Reading the pattern, not the point

When you look at a week of readings, look for the average and the direction, not the highest number. An average of 134/84 with one morning at 152/90 after a bad night's sleep is a mild picture, not an emergency. An average that drifts up over months is worth a conversation even if no single reading crosses a line.

A difference of a few points between arms is normal. A difference of 15 or more between arms, every time, is worth mentioning to your doctor.

If the bottom number is low, say under 60, and you feel dizzy standing up, mention that too. Treatment can overshoot, especially in older people, and the fix is a smaller dose, not a different life.

## When a reading needs action today

Very high readings are the exception. If your monitor shows 180 or more on top or 120 or more on the bottom, sit quietly for five minutes and take it again. If it is still that high and you have a severe headache, chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, weakness on one side or trouble speaking, that is an emergency and you should go to a hospital. If it is still that high and you feel well, call your doctor the same day.

## About the medicine

If your doctor has prescribed a blood pressure medicine, the readings are how you both know it is working. The medicine does not cure the pressure; it holds it down for as long as it is taken, which is why stopping it because the numbers look good is the commonest way for them to climb back. Take it at the same time each day, keep the readings going, and bring the sheet.
